## 4. CHALLENGES TO THE CONCLUSIONS

**(a) The Huang rumor**

The consensus holds up after Nov 8: the affair is confirmed, and the espionage claim is unproven and rests on anonymous US intelligence sources. The council is too quick to accept two exculpatory framings:

1. **The board's "no reason to believe secrets were compromised" is not independent evidence.** It is a statement by the same board that is running the probe, made before the probe has done any work. Weight it accordingly.

2. **"Officials believe Huang didn't know" is also anonymous, and it now sits beside a prior public statement that proved incomplete.**
   - On Oct 29, NVIDIA said the contacts were "not unusual or unexpected." Huang told Bloomberg the Megaspeed dealings were ordinary business.
   - Both statements are now contradicted by his own admission.
   - The live investor risk is disclosure and liability: securities suits, SEC questions, and the board probe "examining statements" (Fortune, Nov 9).
   - Senators Warren and Banks are separately questioning whether his 2025 statements on diversion misled regulators.
   - Only the geopolitics agent raised this, briefly.

3. **The material governance question is missing.** Alice Huang was an executive at Megaspeed, reportedly NVIDIA's largest Southeast Asian customer, with about $4.8B of purchases and under investigation for diversion. Did a personal relationship with a customer's executive affect allocation, pricing or diversion diligence? That question carries more financial weight than whether she is a spy, and no agent framed it.

4. **Key-person risk is underweighted.** An investor quoted by CNBC raised the possibility of a leave of absence. The House briefing request (respond by Nov 11, appear Nov 18) lands the day after earnings. No agent looked at whether NVIDIA has a succession plan.

5. **The user's version, "affair with a Chinese spy," is substantially accurate as reported.** The council should tell the user plainly that this is not a fringe rumor.

**(b) The thesis that NVIDIA spread rumors to make its buyback cheaper**

The rejection is correct. Several of the supporting arguments are miscomputed or overstated, though, and the council missed the one version of the thesis that is worth watching.

**Arguments that hold up:**
- **Origin:** the story came from the NYT (US intelligence sources), and the damaging confirmation came from Huang himself.
- **Sector-wide timing:** most of the drawdown ($239 to $181.60) came by Nov 5, was sector-wide, and peers fell more.
- **Bought near the highs:** NVIDIA bought about $14B in Aug–Sep 2026 at roughly $200–239. A company planning to depress its price would not buy heavily at the top.
- **Insider losses:** Huang's paper loss of about $55B exceeds any buyback "saving" by orders of magnitude.

**Fixes needed:**
- Arguments built on $99B (fundamentals, competition) must use $235B.
- At $235B spread through January 2028, the implied pace is about $25–30B a quarter, or about $0.4–0.5B a trading day. That is still only about 2–3% of daily dollar volume, so the conclusion is unchanged.
- The pre-earnings blackout and 10b5-1 argument is unverified for NVIDIA. Present it as a possibility, not a fact.

**The version of the thesis that deserves monitoring, which no agent raised:**
- The question is not whether NVIDIA planted rumors. It is whether NVIDIA repurchased shares while insiders held material non-public negative information, for example knowledge of the affair before the Nov 8 disclosure.
- Buying while in possession of adverse MNPI (material non-public information) benefits the company at the expense of selling shareholders. It is a recognized Rule 10b-5 concern.
- The timing of disclosure therefore matters. When did Huang tell the board, and did open-market purchases continue between Oct 29 and Nov 8?
- The data to check: the Q3 10-Q, around late November, covers only through Oct 25. The November monthly repurchase table will appear only in the FY27 10-K, around Feb–Mar 2027. Track both.
